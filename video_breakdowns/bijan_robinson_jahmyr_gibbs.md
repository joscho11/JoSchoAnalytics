# Bijan Robinson vs. Jahmyr Gibbs — Comparing Two 2025 Seasons

*2025 season review · RB, Atlanta Falcons / Detroit Lions*

**The one-liner:** Two elite 2025 seasons built from opposite materials. Robinson produced more yardage per game and per touch; Gibbs scored more often per game and per touch and broke far more long runs. This write-up lays out every measurement the video used, the cohort each rank comes from, and the places where the comparison genuinely does not resolve.

---

## 1. The two seasons, side by side

Both played all 17 games.

| 2025, regular season | Robinson | Gibbs |
|---|---:|---:|
| Carries | 287 | 243 |
| Rushing yards | 1,478 | 1,223 |
| Receptions | 79 | 77 |
| Receiving yards | 820 | 616 |
| **Scrimmage yards** | **2,298** | **1,839** |
| Touches | 366 | 320 |
| Rushing + receiving TD | 11 | 18 |

Robinson's 2,298 scrimmage yards led the NFL and are the **12th-largest single season in league history** — a figure reported by NFL.com and the Falcons, not computed here. It is also the only 17-game season in that all-time top 12, which is why every comparison below is expressed as a rate.

## 2. The cohort behind every rank

One panel is used for all historical ranks in the video, and it is worth stating precisely:

> Seasons **2016–2025**, regular season, running backs, one row per player-season, **at least 12 games** AND **at least 10 touches per game**. **n = 366.** Ranked descending; ties share the better rank.

The touches-per-game floor keeps the comparison to backs carrying a real workload. Dropping it to zero widens the panel to 691 seasons and moves none of the ranks below by more than three places.

## 3. Per game

The per-game comparison splits: Robinson's yardage rate is a top-ten season of the last decade, and Gibbs's scoring rate is a top-25 one.

## 4. Per touch

| Rate | Robinson | Gibbs |
|---|---:|---:|
| Scrimmage yards / touch | **6.28** | 5.75 |
| Touchdowns / touch | 3.01% | **5.63%** |

Changing the denominator moves the two gaps in opposite directions. On yardage, Gibbs trails by **20%** per game but only **8.5%** per touch — opportunity accounts for much of that difference, though not all of it. On scoring, Gibbs's lead **widens** from 64% per game to 87% per touch: roughly one score every 18 touches against one every 33.

Both rates are role-sensitive. Touch types, defensive fronts and team scoring environments differ, and neither figure isolates a player from his situation.

## 5. Contact and the passing game

From the public PFF player pages for the 2025 season:

- **Forced missed tackles as a runner:** Robinson **86** (a league high), Gibbs 62
- **Yards after contact per attempt:** Robinson **4.0**, Gibbs 3.0

Paired with the receiving rank in section 3, these are the measurements behind the observation that Robinson's yardage advantage was not only workload.

## 6. The shape of the carries

From nflverse play-by-play, non-scramble rushes only. The denominators reconcile exactly to the official carry counts of 287 and 243.

| Outcome | Robinson | Gibbs |
|---|---:|---:|
| Gained 2 yards or fewer | 35.2% | 43.6% |
| Median gain | 3 yards | 3 yards |
| Reached 10+ yards | **12.54%** | 11.11% |
| Reached 20+ yards | 2.79% | **4.12%** |
| Reached 40+ yards | 0.70% | **2.47%** |

The advantage changes hands at **12 yards**. Robinson reached ten yards on a larger share of carries; from twelve yards upward Gibbs leads at every threshold, and at 40+ his rate is **3.5 times** Robinson's.

**A correction worth making explicitly.** Read against Robinson alone, Gibbs's 43.6% short-carry rate looks like a boom-or-bust profile. Read against the full 366-season panel, it isn't: the cohort **median is 42.9%**, which places Gibbs **166th** — ordinary. The genuine outlier on that axis is Robinson at **345th of 366**; only 21 qualified seasons in the last decade were more consistent. Gibbs is exceptional on the other axis, not this one.

His 40+ rate is **2nd of all 366 seasons**, behind only Saquon Barkley's 2018. Robinson ranks 123rd, and **143 of the 366 seasons never produced a 40-yard run at all.**

One measurement resists the boom-or-bust reading entirely: both backs converted a first down on essentially the same share of carries — **23.3%** for Robinson, **23.9%** for Gibbs. On roughly 250 carries apiece that difference is noise, not a separation.

## 7. Context that complicates the comparison

Neither back operated alone, and the two offenses were not the same size.

- **Both shared a backfield.** Tyler Allgeier and David Montgomery each scored eight rushing touchdowns in 2025.
- **Goal-line work was split.** Inside Atlanta's 10-yard line, Allgeier out-carried Robinson 19 to 18 and outscored him six rushing touchdowns to four.
- **The scoring environments differed sharply.** Detroit produced **56** rushing and receiving touchdowns; Atlanta produced **36**. The two backs accounted for near-identical shares of their own teams — Gibbs **32.1%**, Robinson **30.6%**.

These are descriptive facts about circumstance. They are not a causal account of either player's production, and nothing here establishes what either back would have done in the other's offense.

## 8. Reading the numbers honestly

Four things worth holding onto while you weigh them:

- **The per-touch rates are role-sensitive.** They describe outcomes, not isolated ability — touch types, defensive fronts and team scoring environments all differ.
- **The carry distribution describes outcomes only.** It supports no claim about intent, effort or running style; a distribution cannot show what a runner was trying to do.
- **The first-down rates are tied.** 23.9% against 23.3% on roughly 250 carries apiece is noise, not an advantage.
- **Touchdown totals are among the least stable season-to-season measurements in football** — worth remembering before treating an 18-to-11 gap as a fixed property of either player.

Everything above describes the 2025 season as it happened. None of it is a forecast.
