# Graphs of the Week 02: NFL Week 4

*2026 · Week 4 · four stats with the right comparison*

**The one-liner:** Four Week 4 graphs put a headline in context: how Walker's rushing start compares with other high-volume starts, how McMillan's targets translated into yards, how Minnesota and Houston combine on offense and defense, and how often quarterbacks faced pressure versus what happened on those plays.

---

## 1. Walker's first four weeks, by attempts and yards

The comparison covers **92 running back season starts** from 2012 through 2026 that reached at least 70 rushing attempts in the first four regular-season weeks. Kenneth Walker has **537 yards on 87 carries** (6.17 yards per carry). DeMarco Murray's 2014 start is next by yards, at **534 on 99 carries** (5.39 per carry). Walker's margin is three yards, or about **0.6%**, on 12 fewer attempts.

The attempt axis matters: the chart shows both workload and output instead of comparing yardage alone. Walker's mark is the highest yardage total in this 92-start cohort, and his yards per carry is 0.78 higher than Murray's.

**Limit:** The 70-attempt cutoff selects high-volume starts. The window begins in 2012; Arian Foster's 2010 start also reached 537 yards, but it is outside this 15-season cohort. A separate reported 539-yard Walker figure remains unresolved, so the chart uses the consistent-source total of 537.

## 2. McMillan's Week 4 receiving payoff

The receiving cloud contains **1,017 targeted player-games** from 2026 regular-season Weeks 1–4. Each point is one player in one game; bubble area represents targets. McMillan's Week 4 game produced **192 yards on 16 targets**, or **12.0 yards per target**, with 14 catches (87.5%). Among the 61 player-games with at least 10 targets, that ranked seventh; six were higher.

The three Week 4 examples put that rate beside workload and catches:

- **Tetairoa McMillan:** 192 yards, 16 targets, 12.0 yards per target, 87.5% caught.
- **CeeDee Lamb:** 189 yards, 21 targets, 9.0 yards per target, 81.0% caught.
- **T.J. Hockenson:** 119 yards, 13 targets, 9.2 yards per target, 100% caught.

McMillan's yards per target was **33.3% higher than Lamb's** in these Week 4 games. This is a comparison of single-game output, not an explanation of why it happened or a prediction of future performance.

## 3. Team wins and combined EPA

The team chart compares all **32 teams through four games**. Net EPA per play is offensive EPA per play minus the EPA per play allowed by the defense. EPA estimates how much a play changed a team's expected points, given the game situation.

San Francisco leads at **+0.267 net EPA per play**; Miami is last at **−0.236**. Minnesota is 4–0 with **+0.125** net EPA, seventh in the league. Its offense is 28th at −0.084 while its defense ranks first at −0.209 EPA allowed per play. Houston is 0–4 with **−0.094** net EPA, 25th; its offense ranks 20th (−0.030), and its defense 25th (+0.064 allowed per play).

**Limit:** Four games are a short sample, and the comparison is not opponent-adjusted. EPA and wins describe team results together; this chart alone cannot establish that one unit caused the record.

## 4. Quarterback pressure: exposure and outcome

The pressure comparison includes **27 quarterbacks** who had at least two games, 50 total dropbacks, and 20 pressured plays. Pressure rate is pressured plays divided by dropbacks. The denominator counts passes, sacks, and scrambles once and excludes designed runs. SumerSports supplies the pressured-play split and EPA; nflverse supplies the dropback counts. The definitions reconciled with **zero denominator mismatches**.

Bryce Young faced pressure on **52.0%** of his dropbacks and averaged **−0.02 EPA per pressured play**. Jacoby Brissett's rate was **33.3%**, with **−0.49 EPA per pressured play**. The chart also highlights the pressure-play EPA extremes: Caleb Williams at **+0.35** and Baker Mayfield at **−0.88**. Mahomes and Allen provide additional comparisons.

Pressure rate describes exposure; EPA per pressured play describes the average outcome of those plays. The two measures are not interchangeable. These four-week figures are not opponent-adjusted and do not establish what caused an outcome or predict future performance.
